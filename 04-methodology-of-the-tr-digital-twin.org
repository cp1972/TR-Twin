* 9. Methodology: theories in the simulator — hidden assumptions and falsification

The simulator serves not only to play back real data but also to test theories, by encoding their key arguments as a configuration (chains + controls over time) and observing what they bring about within and between the relational structures. Two uses complement each other: making visible the hidden presuppositions of a theory, and working out its consequences at the level of the relational orders — always in contrast to the Theory of the Relation itself.

** 9.1 The actor (or the instance) as a probe — hypothesis generation

A single actor — or, in the pilot logic of a preliminary study, four to five interviews — can be read as a probe, almost as a test particle in the relational field: it is not the actor for their own sake that is of interest but the field that their trajectory reveals. Where they can pass, where they get stuck, how fast they circulate, are traces of the state of the structures (permeability, satellisation, regime, own reciprocity), measured on the scope of the cohort/probe. Dually: a single mediation instance, perturbed or observed, propagates across the coupled whole (cascade, permeability, satellisation) — it says something about the behaviour of the entire system. This stance is abductive and heuristic, not confirmatory: a single actor under-determines the field. The simulator makes the structural hypotheses that a biography presupposes explicit, explorable, and falsifiable.

** 9.2 Uncovering hidden assumptions — the case of Bourdieu

In the Theory of the Relation, A (Culture) and C (Economy) are relational dynamics far apart from each other: A reaches C only by mediation via D and B, and vice versa — the chains encode exactly this. A is nonetheless an economy, but one that accumulates not authority but the symbolic, prestige: homologous to C in form, different in currency. Bourdieu's peculiar move consists in dialectising the two through the logic of the market — by treating cultural capital as capital, he makes the symbolic directly convertible into the economic (and conversely money into titles). This convertibility short-circuits the mediation via D and B that the Theory of the Relation holds to be essential.

The simulator thereby brings Bourdieu's hidden ontological presupposition before the eyes: one obtains his co-reproduction of cultural and economic capital only if one posits the dialectisation. Put otherwise, the simulator shows in black and white that "Bourdieu = Theory of the Relation + dialectisation of A and C through the market". This is operationalised in the profile "dialectised Bourdieu" (v41): direct A↔C chain coupling at high permeability (see Section 7.1 in the "Usage" document).

** 9.2a A deeper hidden assumption: the state-backed (full) Bourdieu

If one keeps only A and C rigid (the posited premise) and B and D plastic (v43, selective plasticity: a chain declared in the profile is fixed, the others remain mobile), an instructive behaviour shows itself. C captures B before A; A is weakened most strongly precisely at its core instance — the relation (it is disempowered where it is itself, and forced into the logic of C), its own reciprocity is the lowest, and its contribution to global inequality is the second highest after C. Plastic B and D are satellised more slowly (they dodge by reorienting their chain) but exhaust themselves and shrink faster, especially B.

It is tempting to read this as follows: convertibility runs via a converter B, which operates an education and science policy in the service of C — the materially rich adorn themselves with educational titles, a social rupture that is accepted only because politics secretly plays the game of the economy.

Here methodological caution is required: the model encodes A↔C directly (B is only the common second link of A and C, not a legitimator). That B is captured before A is an emergent consequence of this position, not a mechanism in which B authenticates the conversion. The reading "B = the state that legitimises convertibility" is therefore projected onto the simulation; it is not yet contained in the model.

This very projection, however, names the next step — and a deeper hidden assumption of Bourdieu: educational titles are convertible only because the state certifies them. A full, state-backed Bourdieu would not short-circuit the A↔C convertibility across B but would mediate it via B, or tie it to B's backing (B as a bridge A–C, or a conversion term gated by B). The simulator, precisely by not positing B as a converter, makes visible that Bourdieu's doctrine silently needs it: the legitimacy of the dialectisation rests on law and the state. The test to be contrasted would be a profile "state Bourdieu" against the direct dialectisation — planned, not yet implemented.

** 9.3 The falsification discipline

A divergence between simulator and theory informs us about the theory only if the model faithfully encodes the mechanism of the theory — otherwise one tests one's own encoding, not the theory. Hence the method: first grant the theory its premise (e.g. dialectise A and C), then observe. If A now reproduces itself together with C, the model confirms Bourdieu under his own presupposition; if A still depreciates even then, this is an honestly obtained, falsifiable result against Bourdieu. Thus the simulator shifts the question from "true/false" to "which operation must a theory perform in order to pass" — and makes that very operation testable.

** 9.4 An empirical run: English working-class autobiographers as a probe

As a real application, a cohort of 63 English working-class autobiographers (art and media, roughly 1730–1955) was loaded — dated stages from the biographies and from a topic modelling, structure per stage from the activity lexicon, level/category from status indices of the text. The thin cohort is to be read as a probe, not as a representative population.

What it makes visible: in the nineteenth century a contest between Culture (A) and Media (D), both satellised by the Economy (C), the media early on strongly under economic influence; the economy appears as a great source, exporting actors into the other structures. In the twentieth century Politics (B) rises: the actors circulate really and steadily toward it (entries into B rise across the periods), and toward the Second World War B becomes the most influential structure, whose satellites art and media become. Noteworthy and theoretically central: in the late phase D has the largest occupation, yet the model makes B the dominant — domination is precisely not size but relational dynamism (regime, satellisation momentum, category mixture). The probe thus shows a political influence that exceeds the numerical weight.

Methodologically this is the same gesture as in the theory contrast, now on real data: the cohort as a probe lets one read the relational field of an epoch — where paths are open or blocked, who satellises whom — and the circulation flows (Section 7.2 in the "Usage" document) make the carried paths visible as movement (e.g. the increasing entries C→B, D→B, A→B). The findings are heuristically obtained hypotheses, supported by a real circulation, to be held with caution on the thin late phase.

The result outputs (v48) allow this run to be quantified and illustrated print-ready. The internal mobility (category changes within a structure) thereby shows a consistent preponderance of declassing over ascent, most clearly in A and D — within the structures the actors fall more often than they rise, which fits the picture of the culture satellised and depreciated by C.

** 9.5 The sequences as stakes: from macro to micro — and back

Hitherto the cohort orders the actors at the level of the structures (macro) and implicitly assumes that each actor is a good representative of their structure — they carry its central sequence, which is why they can switch from one structure to another. The sequences allow a finer localisation. The four sequences of a structure are not the four instances; they are four ways of seeing, understanding, and defining the stake that the relational order actualises — indexed like g[x][y]: in A the stake is the relation itself, in B its representation in society, in C its concretisation into material things, in D its power of attraction and recruitment.

The four instances (relation, sequence, sequence fragments, control) socialise and maintain the structure — toward the other structures as well as in the inner cohesion of its sequences and fragments; the control instance rebalances when the structure grows or shrinks too much. The sequences, for their part, define the stake. The connection of the two: the instances reinforce the sense — the representation, the definition — that a sequence gives to the relation, as soon as they grow strong. Example: the dialectised Bourdieu reinforces in A a typically economic understanding of culture, because the instances of C in A seek to impose this view — which is consistent, since A is increasingly satellised by C. Satellisation thus reads at the level of the sequences: if A is satellised by C, its C-sequence (materialisation) grows, and A understands itself economically.

For the assignment of actors, four modes of activity serve: A = invest the relation at any price (art for art's sake, unfolding an activity without material or symbolic remuneration, engagement without reservation); B = represent collectives publicly; C = make things concrete (undertake, found publishing houses, issue newspapers); D = make public the merits of the dominant identity, give lectures, recruit by all means. If an actor cannot be clearly assigned, the identity sequence of the structure applies (y=x).

A pilot on the cohort (classification of the complete activity segments) confirms the feasibility: of 219 stages, 82 receive an explicit sequence mode, the rest fall to the identity sequence. The explicit assignments are meaningful: the "socialist propagandist and lecturer" and the "itinerant preacher" become A→D (culture in the attraction and recruitment mode), the member of parliament B→B, the shopkeeper C→C, and above all D→C stands out — journalists who found printing houses or publishers (media whose activity becomes concrete). With this one distinguishes the medium that attracts (D→D) from the medium that materialises (D→C). Three reservations: there is noise (a personal "secretary" is wrongly read as representation); the explicit relation mode A almost never triggers (the "art for art's sake, without remuneration" is rarely explicitly in the text) — which is coherent, since the A actors fall by default onto A, their identity, anyway; and roughly two thirds fall to the default.

From this follows a methodological decision. To feed the living model per year from the sequences would mean filling g[x][y]: 4 structures × 4 sequences = 16 cells for 6–14 active actors — far too thin; the sequence texture would lurch in spikes and become unstable. The sequence layer is therefore kept descriptive and at the actor level, not as a control of the living simulation: the enriched contract table (structure + sequence + category), the sequence transitions in the export (one now reads who switches from materialising to representing, etc.), and an aggregated figure (sequence-stake composition per structure over the whole period, stable because not resolved per year). This is at the same time a generally useful procedure for small samples, as they are frequent in sociology: one refrains from running a thin sample over too many cells and holds the fine structure in an aggregated, descriptive representation.

The decisive point is epistemological. Even if the resolution remains partial because of the few actors, the framework now reaches down to the root of every relational structure: one moves from the macro (the relational structures) to the micro (the actor in their particular sequence within a structure) and likewise from the micro back to the macro. That the reasoning carries in both directions is a clear sign of the solidity of the theoretical framework: the same four stakes that define the structures against one another also order the activities of the individual actors within a structure.
