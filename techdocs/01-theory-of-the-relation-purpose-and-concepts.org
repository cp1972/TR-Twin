* Before you start

This document sets out the theory that the digital twin implements. It proceeds in detail and employs the theory's own vocabulary throughout. Readers unfamiliar with either the theory or the model are advised to begin with [[file:00-start-here.org][document 0]], which states in non-technical terms what the digital twin of the Theory of the Relation is, what it assumes and what may be done with it.

The exposition below is a theoretical argument rather than a description of the software. Where a concept has a counterpart in the model, that correspondence is established in [[file:02-digital-twin-of-the-theory-of-the-relation.org][document 2]], whose mapping table assigns each quantity to the concept it renders.

* Purpose and structure of this documentation

This documentation describes the digital twin of the Theory of the Relation. It pursues two aims: first, to make visible the conceptual bridge between the sociological theory and its computational mechanisms, so that every quantity in the model can be assigned to a theoretical concept; second, to record the development state of the tool, so that later extensions remain traceable.

The digital twin itself pursues three goals. First, it tests the coherence of the theoretical framework of the Theory of the Relation by proposing a runnable simulation of it. Second, it makes it possible to model the relational schemes of other variants of more of less relational theoretical frames in sociology, in order to render visible the contrasts between the Theory of the Relation and those other relational frames. Third, it makes it possible to model real datasets, in order to understand which configurations of the relational orders (relational structures, sequences and sub-sequences, mediation instances, and actor categories) these data bring to light, and to track these configurations over time — whether on the basis of real longitudinal data, or by using the principles of the Theory of the Relation to simulate this evolution and so to build scenarios and to formulate hypothesis about the possible evolution of the data.

The digital twin is a stand-alone web application (HTML/JavaScript, Canvas graphics). It represents four mutually influencing relational structures, each articulated into sequences and into categories of actors, and it makes it possible to feed in real data in order to observe what these data say about the mutual influence of social orders, about the unequal circulation of actors, and about the social inequalities that arise from it.

The following sections describe the theoretical framework.

* Theoretical framework: the Theory of the Relation

The Theory of the Relation conceives the social not /as/ relation but /through/ relation. In place of a reduction of the relation to the position of actors (as the theory works out critically against Bourdieu), it sets a primacy of circulation: society arises from the circulation of actors within relational structures or, like we also say, orders of relations, and this circulation does not run circularly without exception but varies according to the structures in which it takes place.

** Relational structures

The theory conceptualises four relational structures. They are not substantively determined by nature; rather, they derive from one fundamental distinction and its deviations: the distinction between actor-centred (position-oriented) and exchange-centred (relation-oriented) relational structures. From this first distinction of the relational principal feature and its deviations of the first and second order, exactly four structures result. Each relational structure carries the features of the others within it, but subordinated to its own principal feature.

Each relational structure consists of sequences and sub-sequences. Circulation begins and ends, in each structure, in different sequences; from this each structure derives its own specific sequential order. This sequential order distinguishes the four structures from one another.

** Circulation, reciprocity, and resistance

The circulation of actors depends on reciprocity in order to be developed, stabilised, or redirected. Conversely, reciprocity presupposes that actors and collectives circulate. The resistance of actors for and against relational events is the source from which circulation arises. Where circulation is accompanied by monopolisation, individual actors within a sequence can rise to become monopoly actors.

** Actor categories

For each of the four relational structures, four categories of actors can be distinguished, along the difference between morphological determinants (determinants of circulation) and structural determinants (determinants of position):

- *Category 1 — the circulating ("the possessing"):* morphologically determined; free enough from structural constraints to leave one relational structure and switch into another, because they seek a new challenge. They circulate the most.
- *Category 2 — the aspirants:* structurally determined; they support their position rather than circulation and reorganise their position within the same structure (e.g. the move from the novel to the theatre).
- *Category 3 — the conservatives (the "preserving"):* structurally determined; they defend their position and resist devaluation (e.g. the poets who preserve poetry as the purest form of literature).
- *Category 4 — the excluded (the "disappointed"):* morphologically determined; disappointed by a relational structure, they leave it and switch into another. Like Category 1, they are little affected by the structural determinants — but out of disappointment rather than conquest.

In the digital twin, these four categories carry plain-language names: *Established* (Category 1), *Aspirants* (2), *Conservatives* (3), and *Disappointed* (4). The theoretical term appears in each case as a tooltip.

It is noteworthy that both Category 1 and Category 4 are highly mobile: the former circulate through conquest, the latter through disappointment. Categories 2 and 3, by contrast, support position and tend to remain in their structure. The example of Émile Zola — who conquers literature and then leaves it for politics — shows one possible circulation of actors in the first category between relational structures.

** Mobility and stratification

The mobility of actors runs along several axes: vertical (rise and fall between actor categories within the same sequence), horizontal (between the sequences of a relational structure), and between relational structures (change of relational structure). The theory links this mobility to the stratification of the relational structures and to the socialization of actors in relation to social instances. From unequal circulation and declassing, social inequalities arise.

** Mediation instances

Mediation instances (institutions, organisations) support actors, socialise them, and sanction them positively as well as negatively. Every sanction redirects the circulation of actors: one passes and advances, one is rejected and must start over, or one leaves one circulation to pass into another — and may, in the end, support a relational structure other than the one it came from. Unlike actors, instances do not circulate: they grow stronger or weaker and may even be destroyed, depending on whether they succeed in taking in, socialising, and sanctioning more actors. Their strengthening reinforces reciprocity (well-socialised actors, fluid circulation); their decay weakens the sequences and relational structures in which they sit.

The theory distinguishes four kinds of instances, differing in their reach:

- *Instance of the relation:* handles the problems that destabilise the whole structure — there it is powerful; its power decreases the more specific the problem becomes.
- *Instance of the sequence:* specialised in its sequence and very powerful there, weaker on broader (structure-wide) or narrower (sub-sequential) problems.
- *Instance of the sub-sequence:* powerful at the transitions of a sequence — the joints of the social elevator (Established↔Aspirants, Conservatives↔Disappointed), where entrants and leavers are sanctioned — and weak at all other levels.
- *Redistributing instance (control):* does not handle the problems directly but supports all the other instances — strengthening those growing weaker, regulating the over-powerful.

*Decreasing reach.* The force of the instances decreases: relation > sequence > sub-sequence. To socialise a whole sequence is a heavier load and demands more means than to socialise only at the joints (entrants/leavers). The three substantial instances can overlap one another and become satellites, absorb one another as the relational structure grows, or stand in for one another as the relational structure weakens, in order to maintain its dynamism.

*Profile per structure.* The instances are not equally strong in every structure. Dominant is: in A (Culture) the relation, in B (Politics) the sequence, in C (Economy) the sub-sequence, in D (Media) control. Each structure thus receives its own profile — and becomes vulnerable precisely where its strength lies.

*Vulnerable where the strength lies (Pareto).* This follows a mechanism in the sense of Vilfredo Pareto's ophelimity. As the dominant instance grows ever stronger, it socialises and houses ever more actors and thereby drives the expansion of the structure — up to an optimum (the viability maximum). Beyond this optimum, growth turns against the structure itself: sclerosis, fatigue, rupture. Strength becomes its own weakness.

*Degraded relay and control.* A force deficit of one instance is not compensated by another (it is not an average): rather, the immediately subordinate instance takes over the relay — it first grows stronger by stepping in, but then exhausts itself, because it acts at too narrow a level and cannot solve the structure's problem. Thus the load travels downward — from relation to sequence to sub-sequence — and finally calls the redistributing control instance onto the scene, which restores the force of the others. Without this re-regulation a vicious circle arises: the subordinate instances lose their connection to the superordinate ones and fail in their turn. So Gustav Schmoller described it for the economy: large industry does not simply make crafts disappear, because it does not solve the problems of the more local level; in a crisis the crafts can temporarily step in and revive the economy, but they cannot rehabilitate it — without a regulating intervention the economy keeps sinking, destroys jobs, and the crafts too, losing their connection to the larger instances, perish.

** Transversal instances and acts of naming

Since an instance does not circulate, it cannot be in several structures at once. An apparently transversal institution is therefore either an instance anchored in a single structure whose reach is carried by the socialised actors (who, for their part, migrate into other structures), or a coarse collective label for a family of distinct instances, each anchored in a different structure and at a different level. The unit of analysis is not the institution (the "school", the "law"), but the instance, defined by the circulation it mediates, its level, and its substantial or control character. Disentangled in this way, transversality vanishes — and this is a strength of the theory: it compels us to break down the habitual institutional categories.

*The general-education school.* The primary school socialises first toward culture — it is an instance of the relation of A, at the widest level, upstream of everything. Its function of selection and distribution (channelling actors into different circulations, sequences, and relational structures) is not its essence but sets in later and intensifies progressively — at the transition to secondary school, at entrance examinations, at university — the more the educational pathways specialise and the circulations become precise. This selection is exactly the sanction of the sub-sequence instance at the joints (pass / rejected / leave); it gains force by descending from the relation to the sequence and to the sub-sequence. Likewise the educational instances of the other structures arise: they come from the general instance, carried by the circulation of actors (a business school becomes a sequence instance of C, a law faculty one of B).

*The law.* Law at the fundamental level (constitutional rights, jurisprudence, natural law) is a control instance of B — it regulates the functioning of the other instances and is bound to the monopoly of legitimate force (Weber). It specialises and propagates, carried by jurists who bear it, through their circulation, into other relational structures. According to the view of instances in the Theory of the Relation, this is not the same instance spreading but the genesis of new, structure-specific instances (commercial law as an instance of C, etc.), genealogically derived from the basic law of B. Propagation means generation, not migration.

*Institutional isomorphism.* This propagation of well-established instances across different relational orders and their sequences meets a central thesis of organisational sociology: the institutional isomorphism of Paul DiMaggio and Walter Powell (/The Iron Cage Revisited/, 1983), according to which the organisations of a field grow ever more similar to one another over time. They distinguish three mechanisms — coercive, mimetic, normative. The normative mechanism coincides especially closely with the view held here: it is the specialists (professions) who — through their circulation, their training, and their networks — carry established forms from one order into another. In the terms of the Theory of the Relation, this isomorphism is nothing other than the genesis of new, structure-specific instances based on already well-anchored instances, carried by the circulation of actors. The theory thereby gives it a circulatory micro-foundation. Notably, DiMaggio himself comes from the relational network sociology around Harrison White (White was among his teachers). So the convergence is not accidental but a shared relational ancestry. One qualification remains: the "field" in DiMaggio and Powell does not coincide with the relational order. A field corresponds rather to a sequence or to a cross-structural profession. The diffusion mechanism, however, is the same.

*"Civil society".* It names no relational structure — it has neither its own circulation nor its own instances and fails the structure test. It is not a thing but an act of naming. Whoever says "civil society" names the totality of actors (of their own and of other relational structures) and seeks to fix the circulation state of society in order to give more force to their own circulation. In the negative this betrays a difficulty in circulating. The appeal to the collective compensates for one's own movement that is too weak. The theory does not deny the phenomenon that Hegel (civil society), Gramsci (terrain of hegemony), or Habermas (public sphere) name. It shifts it from substance to a positioned act and states that the concept means different things according to the speaker's position.

** The physical fate: the Pareto coupling

Up to this stage, mediation governs the legitimacy of society. This is the reciprocity measured as a curve. It does not yet decide, however, the physical fate of the structures: their size, their expansion at the expense of the others, and their possible disintegration. It is precisely this coupling that the present stage closes. Mediation and morphology now mesh in two directions, i. e.  a descending one (from mediation to size) and an ascending one (from local force to structure-wide expansion).

*** The descending coupling: mediation decides the disintegration

A structure does not fatigue and disintegrate according to a fixed natural law, but to the extent that its instances handle, or fail to handle, the problems that destabilise it. If the cascade — control included — resolves the structural problem, it dampens the fatigue. If an unregulated remainder persists, it accelerates it. The same over-power thus leads, depending on the mediation performance, to very different fates. If it is well mediated, the relational structure remains viable. Poorly mediated, it tips into sclerosis and rupture. This is the sociological point against any mechanical determinism: the fate of a relational structure is not inscribed in its size but in its capacity to mediate itself, i. e. in its relationality. The mechanism is close to the unanticipated consequences of purposive action (Merton 1936) and to perverse effects (Boudon 1977): the effect of an instance turns into its opposite as soon as the level at which it acts is no longer adequate to the problem.

*Internal legitimacy is not systemic reciprocity.* An essential qualification: successful mediation does not automatically raise systemic reciprocity. In a structure in which the relation dominates (A), it rather consolidates the hierarchy. A well-regulated domain stays stratified: it takes in many and socialises them effectively — and thereby distributes them onto unequal positions. Everyone may enter the kitchen; not everyone becomes head chef. The theory therefore distinguishes the internal legitimacy of a structure (its instances function) from systemic reciprocity (circulation between the structures stays fluid and little unequal). The two do not coincide. An internally highly legitimate, well-mediated structure can at the same time consolidate a stable inequality.

*** The ascending coupling: the Pareto loop

The second direction leads from the dominant instance to the expansion of the structure and closes the proper Pareto loop announced earlier as the mechanism of vulnerability. As the dominant instance grows stronger, it socialises and houses ever more actors and thereby drives the growth of the structure at first toward its viability optimum, the bell maximum. Up to there, growth is healthy. Beyond the optimum, however, the same drive turns against the structure. Oversize produces sclerosis and fatigue, strength becomes weakness. This is ophelimity in the sense of Vilfredo Pareto (/Manuel d'économie politique/, 1909): the point at which additional expansion no longer increases but diminishes the total utility. In the model, expansion is therefore triggered only above a threshold. The perfect, optimal symmetric state does not expand of itself. Only the dominant instance driven past the optimum produces the expansive pressure.

*The ascending coupling and its weakness in C.* A relational structure consolidates if the force produced at the lower levels rises upward: from the joint (sub-sequence) to the sequence, from the sequence to the relation. This ascending coupling is not equally strong in all structures. Where it is vigorous, local strength is converted into structure-wide consolidation. Where it is weak — the inertia of small, local instances that fail to find their connection to the superordinate ones — the force stays trapped at the bottom. The structure is strong at its joints and weak as a whole. This is exactly what characterises the economy (C): its strength sits in the sub-sequence (the foundings, the individual successes) but rises with difficulty which explains its volatility.

*Control, strongest in D.* A structure in which control dominates does not expand of itself and regulates itself best. It approaches the control society in the sense of Gilles Deleuze (/Post-scriptum sur les sociétés de contrôle/, 1990), which is a regulation that continuously modulates rather than confines.

** Four diverging fates

From the same mechanisms — profiled instances, ascending coupling, threshold expansion, control — we obtain four markedly different fates for the four structures. They were verified headless (Node.js): the four structures diverge, while the perfect, symmetric state is preserved (equally large relational structures, reciprocity ~1). The following reading is an applied interpretation of the four relational structures; the mechanics itself is general an not bound to the content i.e. the meaning of the relational structures.

- *A · Culture (relation dominant) — open, but stably stratified.* Nourished by migration, it does not expand of itself and dampens its problems effectively at the level of the relational structure. The well-regulated domain (art, university) takes in many actors but distributes them onto unequal ranks. Good mediation here consolidates the hierarchy, not reciprocity (a well-regulated empire remains an empire).
- *B · Politics (sequence dominant) — monopoly and tension.* Its sequence feeds on its own size. It expands toward hegemon, toward monopoly — tense, with chronic ruptures. Here the tendency toward monopolisation of the legitimate order appears (Weber: monopoly of legitimate force) and the instability that follows oversize.
- *C · Economy (sub-sequence dominant) — the most volatile structure.* Its force sits below (strong sub-sequence: foundings, individual ascents; weak relation and sequence, weak collectives), and the ascending coupling is weak. From this follows the boom-bust cycle: rapid ascent of individuals, equally rapid disintegration, without lasting structure-wide consolidation. This mimic the creative destruction in the sense of Schumpeter (1942), and finds its best example in the figure of the self-made man.
- *D · Media (control dominant) — best regulated.* Control reigns; the structure does not expand of itself and regulates itself best. This is the control society in the sense of Deleuze. From the perspective of the dominant position it appears as a site of achievement and singularity (Reckwitz, /Die Gesellschaft der Singularitäten/, 2017). The nuance remains: it regulates without emancipating (stability is not reciprocity).

These four fates are not value judgements about the relational structures but consequences of the profiles of their instances. They show out of one single mechanics which is the same for all relational structures, how diverging morphological trajectories arise, depending on which instance dominates and how vigorously the force rises from below to above.

** Satellisation: the second distress of the structures

Up to this point the model knew only a single distress: that of over-expansion (the Pareto loop described above). This, however, captured inequality and reciprocity solely from the perspective of the expanding structure. The shrunken, marginalised structure registered nothing. That was a gap. Satellisation closes this gap. It is the second, opposite distress.

*The thesis.* A relational structure that shrinks does not merely become small. It (a) loses internal solidity: its starved instances can no longer carry it autonomously; (b) it becomes a satellite of the structure that satellises it: it orbits the dominant structure, becomes dependent on it, moves closer to it, and can finally enter it. This is, at the level of relational structures, the counterpart to the metropolis-satellite or centre-periphery relation as described by dependency and world-systems theory (Frank 1967; Wallerstein 1974). The periphery is not simply "less developed" but it is actively made dependent by the centre. Likewise here, the marginalised relational structure is actively satellised by the dominant one.

Satellisation has three aspects.

- *Registered distress.* The satellised structure now receives a problem that engages its cascade and its control: it mobilises against marginalisation. But its starved instances do not solve it: the remainder stays high, reciprocity falls. The satellite cannot free itself by its own force.
- *Loss of internal solidity.* The energy of its instances erodes. It is a loss of internal solidarity (Durkheim 1893) distinct from mere flow shortage. But there is no destruction of a relational structure. The relation erodes deeply, yet as soon as the structure is freed it rebuilds itself over time. Re-emancipation is possible. It takes time, but it occurs (verified headless: the relation returns, after liberation, from ~0.25 to ~0.69).
- *Active satellisation.* The satellite is actively absorbed. It shrinks further in favour of the dominant, to which its mass passes, and at the same time moves closer to it, until it almost touches it or enters it. Both, however, are capped. It shrinks without vanishing (lower bound), and its orbit approaches continuously without overlapping.

*Reciprocity becomes two-sided.* With satellisation, both poles become visible at once. We can observe the over-expanded structure (Pareto distress) and the marginalised one (satellisation distress). Inequality and reciprocity are no longer read only from the perspective of expansion. With this, the symmetry of the relational reading is restored. One and the same inequality appears from above (over-power) and from below (dependency).

Like the Pareto coupling, satellisation too does not touch the perfect state. It is the product of a deficit and a domination, both of which are zero at the symmetric optimum. It sleeps as long as nothing dominates, and awakens only when a structure is at once shrunk and dominated.
