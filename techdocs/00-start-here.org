#+OPTIONS: toc:t num:t ^:nil
#+LANGUAGE: en

This document assumes nothing. Not the Theory of the Relation, not the book it
comes from, not the model. Read it first if you have never met either; the
other nine documents go into depth and expect you to have read this one.

* What this is

A digital twin is a runnable copy of something, kept close enough to the
original that you can experiment on the copy instead of the thing. Usually the
original is a machine. Here it is a *sociological theory*.

The Theory of the Relation makes claims about how societies hold together and
come apart: that people circulate between domains of social life, that
institutions channel that circulation, and that inequality arises from the
circulation being unequal. Those claims are stated in prose. Prose can be
consistent-sounding and still contain a contradiction nobody notices.

So the theory was rebuilt as a program. Every claim became a mechanism, every
concept a quantity. You press play and watch what the theory does when it is
made to run. If it produces something absurd, the theory has a problem — and
you can see exactly where.

That is the first use. Two others followed, and they turned out to matter more
in practice: you can encode a *different* theory in the same machinery and
watch where the two diverge; and you can feed the machinery *real data* and let
it read the state of a social field off them. All three are described below.

The twin is one file, =tr-twin.html=. Open it in a browser. No installation, no
server, no account.

* A note on how it was built

This model was built in collaboration with an artificial intelligence. The
theory, the sociological judgements and the decisions about what counts as a
faithful translation are the author's; a large part of the implementation, of
the headless verification and of the documentation was written with AI
assistance.

This is said here for three reasons. It is a fact about the provenance of the
code and belongs in the open. It explains the unusual density of verification
in the sections that follow — every mechanism was checked headless before it
entered the application, which is cheap when a machine writes the harness and
expensive when a person does. And it is itself a claim the reader may want to
test: the code is here, and so are the verification scripts.

* How the twin works, in five minutes

** Four structures

The model divides social life into four *relational structures*. In the
interface they are labelled Culture (A), Politics (B), Economy (C) and Media
(D), but those labels are an applied interpretation, chosen because they make
real data readable. The theory itself derives four structures more abstractly,
from a single distinction — whether a domain organises itself around the
*positions* people hold or around the *exchanges* they enter into — and its
deviations. What matters for using the twin is that there are four, that they
differ in kind and not merely in content, and that each one carries traces of
the other three.

Each structure has a *size*: how much of the whole social field it occupies.
The four sizes always sum to one, so a structure can only grow at another's
expense. That constraint does a lot of work.

** Sequences

Each structure is internally divided into four *sequences* — roughly, modes of
operating within a domain: its core, its way of representing itself, its way of
producing, its way of circulating and controlling. Sixteen sequences in all.

Structures differ in the *order* in which circulation runs through their
sequences. That order — the model calls it the chain — is what distinguishes
one structure from another. Change the chain and you change the structure's
character.

** Actors, in four categories

People are not modelled one by one. Each sequence holds a *mixture* of four
categories:

- *Established* — mobile by conquest. Secure enough to leave a domain for a new
  challenge. They circulate most.
- *Aspirants* — building a position. They move within their domain rather than
  out of it.
- *Conservatives* — defending a position that is being devalued. Over time they
  tend to lose, and become —
- *Disappointed* — mobile by disappointment. They too leave, but downward and
  outward rather than upward.

The pairing is the interesting part: the most mobile categories sit at the top
*and* the bottom. Movement is not the same as success.

** Instances

*Instances* are the institutions that take people in, socialise them and
sanction them — schools, editorial boards, professional bodies, funding
committees. They do not circulate; only actors do. An instance grows stronger
as it successfully processes actors and weakens when it fails.

They come at three reaches — the whole structure, one sequence, the joints
between sequences — plus a fourth kind, *control*, which does not handle
problems itself but restores the others when they exhaust themselves.

** What comes out

Two numbers matter most:

- *Reciprocity* — how fluid the circulation between structures is. High when
  people can move and be received; it falls when a structure closes or when one
  structure swallows the others. It is measured, not set: there is no
  reciprocity dial.
- *Inequality* — a Gini coefficient, computed at three nested levels at once:
  between structures (transversal), between sequences within a structure
  (horizontal), and between actor categories within a sequence (vertical).

Watching the three levels move against each other is where most of the
analytical value sits. They do not move together.

* What the model assumes

Every model buys its results with assumptions. These are the ones this model
makes. You do not have to accept them — but if you use the twin, you are using
them, so they should be visible.

1. *Circulation comes first.* Society is not made of positions that people
   occupy but of movements that people make. Position is a residue of
   circulation, not its ground. This is the theory's principal break with
   Bourdieu, and it is built into the mechanics: the model moves actors and
   reads structure off the movement.

2. *Four structures, not three or seven.* The number follows from one
   distinction and its first- and second-order deviations. If you think a
   domain of your data belongs to none of the four, the model will make you
   argue for that rather than adding a fifth.

3. *Instances do not circulate.* Only actors do. So an institution that appears
   to operate across several structures — a school, a legal system — is either
   anchored in one structure with its reach carried by the people it socialised,
   or it is a coarse label for several distinct instances. The model forces the
   second reading. This is a genuine constraint and occasionally an awkward one.

4. *Strength turns into weakness past an optimum.* A structure's dominant
   instance grows by processing actors, which grows the structure, which is
   healthy — up to a point. Past that point the same growth produces sclerosis.
   Nothing is punished for being weak; things are punished for succeeding too
   long. (After Pareto's notion of ophelimity.)

5. *Deficits are relayed downward, not averaged.* When an instance fails, the
   next narrower one takes over — and exhausts itself, because it acts at too
   local a level to solve a structural problem. The load travels down until
   control intervenes. There is no compensation term, no mean.

6. *A well-run structure is not an equal one.* Internal legitimacy and systemic
   reciprocity are different quantities and can move in opposite directions. A
   domain whose institutions work beautifully may be admitting many people and
   sorting them into stable ranks. The model keeps the two apart deliberately.

7. *The perfect state is symmetric and does nothing.* At start-up all four
   structures are equal, inequality is zero, reciprocity is one. Every mechanism
   in the model is written so that it is *inert* at that state. Nothing happens
   until something is made asymmetric. This is a discipline, not a decoration:
   it means any effect you see has a cause you introduced.

8. *The run is deterministic.* A fixed seed sets the initial perturbation. Same
   settings, same trajectory, every time. The twin is a comparative instrument,
   not a Monte Carlo simulation.

* What you can do with it

** With no data at all

Take a theoretical framework you are reading — Parsons, Luhmann, Habermas,
White, Tilly, Bourdieu — and encode how *it* construes circulation, mediation
and reciprocity as a set of cursor settings. Run it. Compare the end state
against the reference run. The difference is that framework's signature, made
visible rather than argued.

Twenty-four such profiles ship with the twin, in
[[file:../simulation-mode/README.org][simulation-mode/]]. Reading them against
each other is a way of doing theory comparison that does not depend on anyone's
summary of anyone else.

** With a handful of cases

This is the case the twin was extended for, and it is the common one in
qualitative sociology and in student projects: five interviews, twelve
biographies, one archive.

*Prism mode* reads the field through one to five actors treated as *probes* —
test particles whose trajectories reveal the state of the field they moved
through. Which channels were open to them? Where were they stopped? Which
instances certified them? A single trajectory under-determines the field, and
the documentation says so plainly; but a well-chosen handful reproduces, in
miniature, what a full cohort shows. If it does not, that is a finding too.

See [[file:07-prism-mode-actor-probes.org][document 7]] for the method, and
[[file:08-scenario-protocol-for-students.org][document 8]] for a protocol that
takes you from a few interviews to a defensible what-if scenario without
pretending the sample is larger than it is.

** With a full dataset

Feed in a cohort as a table — who, when, which structure, which category, which
sequence — plus the mediation and control instances they passed through. The
twin reads the three levels of inequality off the data and shows how they moved
over time.

The worked example in [[file:../data-mode/README.org][data-mode/]] does this
with 163 English working-class autobiographers between 1790 and 1945, and its
coding pipeline is fully documented, down to the residue the coding could not
resolve.

* What it cannot do

- It does not predict. Scenarios extend a trajectory under stated assumptions;
  that is an argument, not a forecast.
- It does not identify individuals or produce individual-level results. Actors
  enter as distributions; even in prism mode a trajectory is read as evidence
  about the *field*, not about the person.
- It does not settle whether the Theory of the Relation is true. It settles
  whether it is coherent, and it makes its consequences inspectable.
- The four structure labels are an interpretation. If your material does not
  sort into Culture / Politics / Economy / Media, the abstract four still apply
  but you will have to do the mapping yourself and defend it.
